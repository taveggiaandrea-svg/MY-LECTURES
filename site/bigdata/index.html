Why can't we just query the operational database?

The data analytics timeline: from printed reports on operational systems to data warehouses, OLAP, predictive analytics and Big Data platforms.

A company sells through one system and keeps in touch with its clients through another. Both store customers. Ask both the same simple questions and look at what comes back.

Nobody made a mistake. Each system was built for its own job, by its own team, with its own conventions. The trouble appears only when you use the data for something they were not designed for: analysis across the whole company.

The lecture starts from an informal vocabulary, refined in lesson 2. A database is a set of tables and other objects. A table, also called a relation, is a set of records defined by a list of fields (columns), each with a name and a data type. A record is one horizontal line of a table: the values of all the fields for one thing, such as one customer.

Five phases, each one fixing the last

The lecture tells the history of data analysis as five phases. Each adds a layer on top of the previous ones.

From the 1980s, the RDBMS (relational database management system, the software that stores and manages tables) and SQL (Structured Query Language, used to ask tables questions) made it possible to produce reports automatically. They were static, mostly printed, and every query ran on the operational databases.

An operational database stores the day-to-day activity of a company in real time: every order, invoice and payment. It sits under applications such as an ERP (enterprise resource planning: software for accounting, procurement and production, for example SAP or Oracle JD Edwards) and a CRM (customer relationship management: software that tracks every interaction with customers and prospects, for example Salesforce).

What a report is, at three heights

A report is a document that organizes information in narrative, graphic or tabular form, prepared ad hoc, periodically or as required; put simply, the formatted result of database queries that contains useful data for decision making. The lecture distinguishes three kinds by how much they aggregate and how often they are produced.

The higher a report sits in the company, the more it aggregates and the less often it is produced. Operational reports are daily and mix detail with totals; tactical ones are daily or weekly; strategic ones summarize the company's situation weekly or monthly.

Why analysis on operational databases goes wrong

The lecture lists three problems. The first figure showed one of them: the same data live in more than one system, so formats differ (Milano or Milan) and versions differ (an old address next to the new one).

The other two come from design. Operational databases are built for fast inserts and updates of single records, not for reading millions of rows at once. And reports compete with transactions for the same machine: a heavy report slows down the people entering orders, and vice versa.

A separate database just for analysis

From the 1990s companies built an analytical database next to the operational ones: the data warehouse (DWH). At first it was a mere copy, which solved the performance problem. Later the data from different sources were integrated, made consistent, cleansed and reorganized into the dimensional model, which lesson 2 opens up.

Data reach the warehouse through ETL: extract the data from the operational sources, transform them by applying business rules and data quality checks, load them into the warehouse. The same acronym also names the software tools that do it.

The two business rules used here, how names are written and which source wins when ERP and CRM disagree, are examples chosen for this page. Every company writes its own.

Because its data are integrated from all sources and cleansed, the warehouse is called the single source of truth. A data warehouse is designed to support business decisions, by letting you consolidate, analyze and report data at different levels of aggregation.

It is also the starting point of Business Intelligence (BI): the technologies, applications and practices for collecting, integrating, analyzing and presenting business information, with the purpose of better decisions. The BI process runs extract, cleanse, transform and integrate, load the warehouse, then analyze and disseminate. In the lecture's diagram of a BI system, the operational sources (CRM, ERP and legacy systems, older software still in use) feed ETL and a staging area, then the relational warehouse, then a semantic layer such as OLAP (which presents data in business terms, so users need not know the tables), then the client tools people use.

Asking questions without writing SQL

A warehouse still has to be queried in SQL, and the user must know its tables and how they relate. From the late 1990s, OLAP (online analytical processing) engines removed that barrier. They replace tables with multidimensional structures and store data together with metadata, data about the data, such as how tables connect.

So the user no longer needs to know the relationships, and OLAP answers aggregate questions (sums, averages, counts) much faster than a relational database. It is a technology for data discovery: unlimited report viewing, complex analytical calculations, analysis along any dimension. The lecture describes the OLAP client as a huge pivot table over the whole warehouse, used by drag and drop.

Every choice of rows and columns is one view of the same cube. This is the dynamic report of phase 3, as opposed to the static, printed report of phases 1 and 2.

From what happened to what will happen

Warehouse and OLAP both look backwards. From around 2000 companies increasingly wanted to predict future events, such as next month's sales or the cost of energy. A new term appeared, data mining: discovering hidden patterns in large data sets that the business can exploit, for customer segmentation, market basket analysis (which products are bought together), churn analysis (who is about to leave), campaign targeting or demand forecasting.

This splits analytics in two. Descriptive analytics summarizes raw data so humans can read it, and describes the past: static reports and BI belong here. Predictive analytics estimates how likely a future outcome is, using statistics, machine learning and data mining on current and historical facts.

Machine learning, in the lecture's words, is the science of getting computers to act without being explicitly programmed. Data mining borrows from it, from artificial intelligence, from statistics and from database systems; today predictive analytics is largely a modern name for data mining.

Data too big for the old tools

From around 2012 people started talking about Big Data. The usual definition lists three features, the three Vs: volume (potentially huge, such as every card transaction or every reading of an industrial sensor), velocity (fast data arriving continuously, as from Internet of Things sensors) and variety.

The lecture prefers a sharper definition: Big Data are data that we cannot analyze, or that it is not convenient to analyze, with traditional tools such as relational databases. The main tools for them are Hadoop, a distributed file system plus computation engine whose many tools cover the whole data life cycle, from ingestion to presentation, and Spark, a fast distributed computation engine more oriented to analysis; both get their own lectures later.

Variety needs a closer look. Structured data fit conveniently in a table, like ERP data. Unstructured data do not, like the text of a post or an image from medical equipment. Most data called unstructured are actually semi-structured.

A note on the slides: one slide defines unstructured data as data that "can be conveniently put into a table". It is a typo for "cannot", as lesson 4 states correctly.

In the 2020s the trend topics are Big Data again (new versions of Hadoop and Spark), predictive analytics and cloud computing: renting platforms (PaaS, platform as a service) or raw infrastructure (IaaS, infrastructure as a service) instead of buying servers, with resources that scale at low prices. Traditional sources such as ERPs and CRMs now keep a deeper level of detail, and new sources keep arriving: scientific and medical equipment, distributed control systems in plants, high frequency trading, social media, Internet of Things devices.

From informal to formal

A relational database is made of logical units called tables, or relations. It follows the relational model of data proposed by the computer scientist E. F. Codd in 1970, and its data are accessed with SQL. The software that runs it is the RDBMS (relational database management system).

An RDBMS stores data in tables, lets you create new databases and their structures, lets you query and modify data with SQL, keeps vast amounts of data for a long time, recovers the database after failures, errors or misuse, and controls access by many users at once.

A table is defined by its metadata (data about the data, as in lesson 1): a name (Customers), one or more columns (customer_id, first_name, last_name, city) and a primary key, the column whose value identifies each row. Each field has a name, a data type (string, number, date, boolean or binary) and a NULL constraint that says whether it may be left empty; NULL means no value. A record, also called row or tuple, holds the values for a single entity, such as one customer.

Text comes in two main types: CHAR(n) always has length n, VARCHAR(n) has variable length, at most n. Dates use DATE, DATETIME or TIME. Numbers are integers (INT, and in MySQL a family from TINYINT, 1 byte, to BIGINT, 8 bytes), floating point (FLOAT) or fixed decimals: DECIMAL(precision, scale) has precision digits in total, scale of them after the point.

CHAR wastes space on short values but has a fixed size; VARCHAR saves space and pays one extra byte to record the length. A value longer than 4 characters is cut to 4 in both, as in the slide's last row.

Three kinds of integrity

The primary key constraint guarantees that records are unique: insert a duplicate value and the RDBMS returns an error, as in the first panel of the opening figure. This protects entity integrity: no duplicate rows.

Sales does not repeat each customer's name and city; it stores only customer_id, the customer's primary key. Nothing stops you from typing a customer that does not exist, unless you declare a foreign key: a constraint linking Sales.customer_id to Customers.customer_id. A foreign key field can be NULL, or it must contain a value found in the linked primary key. This is referential integrity, at work in the second and fourth panels.

Data types and NULL constraints protect domain integrity: all values of a column belong to the same domain. A CHECK constraint adds any other rule, such as quantity > 0 (third panel). The lecture's full table declares all three:

CREATE TABLE Sales (
    sales_id     INT  PRIMARY KEY NOT NULL,
    sales_date   DATE,
    customer_id  INT  REFERENCES Customers (customer_id),
    product_name VARCHAR(50),
    quantity     INT  CHECK (quantity > 0),
    price        DECIMAL(7, 2)
);

Keys and constraints turn a table from a grid of cells into a set of rows that cannot contradict its own rules. DROP TABLE Sales; deletes both the data and the definition; the lecture drops Sales first, then Customers.

Why some searches are instant

A database holds other objects besides tables: views (saved queries that behave like tables), procedures and functions (saved pieces of SQL code) and indexes. An index is a data structure that speeds up retrieval, like the index of a book. Without one, the RDBMS must scan the entire table even to return a single record.

The lecture gives only the book analogy. The halving search is one simple way an index can work, added here for illustration; real RDBMS use tree structures built on the same idea.

Where errors creep in: normalization

Normalization organizes data by decomposing tables, to eliminate redundancy and the anomalies it causes in inserts, updates and deletes. Its rules are the normal forms: first, second, third, Boyce-Codd, fourth and more; the course covers only the first and the third.

The lecture's first example is a customer table with a duplicate record and two non-atomic fields: Name holds first and last name, Address holds street, city and country. Finding the customers in New York is awkward, because the city is buried inside a longer text.

Normalization stores every fact once, so it cannot disagree with itself. Department name and phone depend on the department code, not on the employee: that transitive dependency is what the split removes.

Two databases, two shapes

OLTP (online transaction processing) databases are the transaction oriented, operational ones of lesson 1. They are fully normalized: many tables, few columns each, no redundancy. Normalization prevents errors in inserts, updates and deletes; the price is that a report must put many tables back together.

A data warehouse is analysis oriented, and its goal is a simple model for the analyst. It has two kinds of tables. Dimensions hold all the attributes of a business entity (customer, product, shop) and are denormalized, in first normal form. Fact tables hold the measures (quantities, values, counts) and references to the dimensions, and are in third normal form.

Each dimension row gets a progressive number created by ETL, the surrogate key (or DWH key), while the original key of the source system, the business key, is kept as an ordinary column. The fact table points to the dimensions through the surrogate keys.

ETL takes care of integrity in the warehouse, so a DWH generally has no foreign keys and often no primary keys. ETL also generates the surrogate keys, applies business rules, integrates sources and checks data quality.

A language that says what, not how

SQL is domain specific: it only applies to databases. It is declarative: you describe the result you want, not the algorithm, and a component of the RDBMS called the optimizer chooses how to get it. Every RDBMS also adds procedural instructions such as IF and WHILE in its own dialect: T-SQL in Microsoft SQL Server, PL/SQL in Oracle, SQL PL in IBM DB2, SQL/PSM in MySQL.

Why learn it in a Big Data course? Because most Big Data and NoSQL tools (the non relational databases of lesson 4) speak it: Hive in Hadoop uses HiveQL, HBase can be queried through Phoenix, Spark has Spark SQL, Cassandra has the CQL dialect, and many tools also offer ODBC drivers, a standard way for programs to connect to a database.

SQL statements fall into four sublanguages: DQL (data query language) to query, DDL (data definition language) to create tables and other objects, DCL (data control language) to grant users permissions, DML (data manipulation language) to insert, update and delete. A query retrieves data and starts with SELECT. A statement modifies data or structure, or controls the program flow. A clause is a part of either, such as WHERE. An expression returns a value, such as price*quantity, and a predicate is a logical condition, such as city = 'Milan'.

In DDL, CREATE TABLE name (column_specification, ...) lists each column with its data type and, optionally, primary key, NULL, REFERENCES and CHECK specifications, as in the Sales table above. In DML, INSERT adds a record, listing the columns or giving values for all of them in order; INSERT … SELECT adds all the rows returned by a query:

INSERT INTO customers VALUES (1, 'john', 'doe', 'london');
INSERT INTO customers SELECT id, first_name, last_name, city FROM temp_customers;

UPDATE changes values with SET, DELETE removes records. Both accept a WHERE clause, and that is where the classic mistake hides.

Without WHERE, UPDATE and DELETE act on every row of the table.

The select list can contain scalar expressions, computed once per row with the operators + (add, or concatenate text), -, *, / and % (modulo), and named with an alias after AS. It can use built in functions: YEAR, MONTH and DAY extract parts of a date (not in SQLite), TRIM removes spaces at both ends, UPPER and LOWER change case, ABS and ROUND work on numbers.        WHEN city IN ('liverpool', 'london') THEN 'UK'
       ELSE 'N/A'
  END AS Country
FROM Customers;
SELECT sales_date, product_name, quantity, price * quantity AS amount
FROM Sales
WHERE product_name = 'xyz'
ORDER BY quantity DESC;

Lesson 3 · A. Rezzani, slides and SQL scripts

How do you combine two tables?

Advanced SQL: UNION, joins, missing values, grouping and summarizing, subqueries and analytical functions.

Lesson 2 ended with normalized tables: every fact stored once, in its own table. The price is that answers are scattered. A sale knows its buyer only by number; the name sits in another table. This lesson puts the pieces back together.

Who bought what?

These are the lecture's two tables. Buyers has a name and a buyer_id; sales has a buyer_id, a product id and a quantity. You want one result with the buyer's name next to each quantity. The most naive way is to pair every buyer with every sale.

The cartesian product is all the possible combinations of rows of two tables, the intermediate result of the FROM clause; the slide's own example crosses 3 names (Davis, Funk, King) with 3 products into 9 rows. It is the starting point of every join, and in SQL queries it is usually not what you want: most pairs match a buyer with someone else's sale.

Join. A join puts the columns of two tables side by side, matching rows with a predicate. A CROSS JOIN returns the whole cartesian product. An INNER JOIN keeps only the pairs where the ON predicate is true. An outer join also preserves the rows of a designated table and fills the missing side with NULL: LEFT keeps every row of the first table, RIGHT every row of the second, FULL both.

SELECT buyer_name, sales.buyer_id, qty
FROM buyers LEFT OUTER JOIN sales
  ON buyers.buyer_id = sales.buyer_id;

An outer join is an inner join plus the rows that found no partner, padded with NULL. Lucy Thin bought nothing: she disappears from INNER and RIGHT, and survives with NULLs in LEFT and FULL.

Stacking instead of matching: UNION

A join adds columns. UNION adds rows: it combines the results of two or more SELECT statements with the same columns, one under the other, and removes duplicate rows. UNION ALL does the same but keeps the duplicates. The course script uses t1 = {(1, 'a'), (2, 'b')} and t2 = {(1, 'a'), (3, 'c')}.

A practical note added on this page: removing duplicates means comparing rows, which costs work, so when the two results cannot overlap UNION ALL gives the same rows faster.

When a value is missing

The outer joins produced NULLs, and SQL treats them in a special way. With no missing values a predicate is TRUE or FALSE: 5 > 2 is TRUE. With missing values there is a third outcome, UNKNOWN: comparing NULL to anything, another NULL included, gives UNKNOWN.

Query filters (ON, WHERE, HAVING) keep only TRUE, so UNKNOWN rows are silently dropped. That is why you test for missing values with IS NULL or IS NOT NULL, never with = NULL. The function COALESCE(x, 'N/A') replaces a NULL with a value of your choice: in the lecture's query on this left join, Bianchi's row shows product 'N/A' and quantity 0 instead of two NULLs.

From rows to groups

An aggregate function turns many rows into one value: SUM, MIN, MAX, AVG, COUNT, and the standard deviations and variances STDEV, STDEVP, VAR, VARP. They return a single value with no column name, can be used in SELECT, HAVING and ORDER BY, and ignore NULLs, except COUNT(*), which counts rows. Alone they summarize the whole table; with GROUP BY they summarize each group, one output row per unique combination of the grouping columns.

The order matters. WHERE filters rows before aggregation, and after GROUP BY the detail rows are lost: only the groups remain. To filter groups you need a clause that runs after GROUP BY and can test an aggregate: HAVING.

The next examples use the course's OrdersDB script: a Customer table (Anders, Trujillo, Moreno), an Orders table with OrderDate, CustomerId and TotalAmount, an OrderItem table with the quantity of each product in each order, and a Product table. Without GROUP BY, SELECT SUM(i.quantity), COUNT(*) FROM OrderItem i returns one row: 240 units over 16 items. Grouped by product, it returns one row per product: Chai 48 units in 5 items, Chang 71 in 4, Aniseed Syrup 56 in 4, Chef Anton's Cajun Seasoning 65 in 3.

SELECT p.productname, SUM(i.quantity) TotalQt, COUNT(*) nr_of_sales
FROM OrderItem i JOIN Product p ON p.id = i.productid
GROUP BY p.productname
HAVING SUM(i.quantity) > 60;

With no WHERE, only Chang (71) and Chef Anton's Cajun Seasoning (65) pass HAVING, the lecture's result. COUNT(DISTINCT customerid) on Orders counts different values instead of rows: 2, since only Anders and Trujillo placed orders.

A query inside a query

A subquery is a query nested in another. Its result is passed to the outer query, which uses it like an expression. A subquery can return one value (scalar), a list, or a whole table. It is self-contained if it does not depend on the outer query, and correlated if it uses values from the outer query's current row.

WHERE o.orderdate = (SELECT MAX(orderdate) FROM Orders)                     -- self-contained
WHERE o.orderdate = (SELECT MAX(orderdate) FROM Orders o2
                     WHERE o2.customerid = o.customerid)              -- correlated

The self-contained version answers "who placed the last order", one row. The correlated version answers "when did each customer place their last order", one row per customer. With EXISTS a subquery becomes an existence test: it is TRUE if the subquery returns at least one row, FALSE otherwise. In the course database WHERE EXISTS (SELECT * FROM Orders o WHERE o.customerid = c.id) returns Anders and Trujillo, and NOT EXISTS returns Moreno, the customer with no orders.

Aggregating without losing the rows

GROUP BY collapses rows. Analytical functions compute over groups of rows but keep every row, adding the result as a new column. They use the OVER clause, made of PARTITION BY, which splits the rows into partitions (one per customer, say), and ORDER BY, which orders the rows inside each partition.

On top of that you can set a window: which rows around the current one enter the calculation. UNBOUNDED PRECEDING starts at the first row of the partition, n PRECEDING n rows before the current one, CURRENT ROW at the current row; n FOLLOWING and UNBOUNDED FOLLOWING look ahead.

The moving average uses AVG(TotalAmount) OVER (PARTITION BY customerid ORDER BY OrderDate ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW). Anders has two orders on 5 July, and the slide lists 1813 before 670.8; this page keeps that order. For his third order, 670.8, the window holds his first three orders:

\[ \frac{440 + 1813 + 670.8}{3} = 974.6 \]

The running total uses SUM from UNBOUNDED PRECEDING. SUM(TotalAmount) OVER (), with nothing in the brackets, puts the grand total 13595.5 on every row; OVER (PARTITION BY customerid) puts each customer's total instead, 4066.8 for Anders and 9528.7 for Trujillo. With ROW_NUMBER ordered by TotalAmount descending, keeping rn = 1 finds each customer's largest order. LEAD works like LAG but looks n rows ahead.

Lesson 4 · A. Rezzani, slides

What if one machine is not enough?

The Big Data toolbox: distributed systems, NoSQL databases, ACID and BASE, columnar compression, graph and vector databases.

Lessons 2 and 3 lived inside one relational database on one server. Lesson 1 already named the limit: Big Data are data we cannot analyze, or cannot analyze at a reasonable cost, with a traditional RDBMS. Recall the three Vs: volume, velocity and variety. Unstructured data have no fields or columns and cannot conveniently go into a table: documents (Word, Excel, PowerPoint, PDF), images, e-mails, Web 2.0 content (blog posts, forums, wikis). For huge amounts of data, unstructured data and distributed calculations, does a system exist that is also easy to use? This lesson starts the answer.

Many small machines instead of one big one

For huge amounts of data you need more than one machine: a distributed system that splits the data among its servers and computes on all of them at once. A node is a single server, a rack is a case holding a bunch of servers, and a cluster is a collection of servers, in one or more racks, that behaves as if it were a single machine.

This is horizontal scalability: you add servers to gain computing power and storage, instead of buying a bigger one. Unstructured data add a second need, systems that work with non tabular structures; complex calculations such as machine learning add a third, doing them in a distributed way. And all of it should stay easy for the final user.

The two main tools are Hadoop, a complete Big Data platform (lesson 5), and Spark, a high-performance calculation engine (lessons 6 and 7). This lesson covers the third kind of tool: NoSQL databases.

What NoSQL gives up

NoSQL databases deviate from the rules of relational databases. They are distributed, mostly open source, horizontally scalable and schema free: there is no table definition, and they can host data whose structure varies from record to record. Their programming interface is simple, and most of them end up offering an SQL interface anyway.

What they generally do not guarantee are the ACID properties of transactions. A transaction is a set of commands, reads or changes, that must run with all or nothing logic. Take a transfer of 100 euro from account A to account B: a withdrawal and a deposit, and you never want only one of them (this example is added on this page).

ACID. The slide names the four properties; the one-line meanings are added here. Atomicity: all or nothing. Consistency: the database moves from one valid state to another. Isolation: concurrent transactions do not see each other's half-done work. Durability: once confirmed, a change survives failures.

Giving up ACID can look like a serious flaw that breeds inconsistencies. Many NoSQL systems follow the BASE model instead: basically available (the application always answers), soft state (it does not have to be consistent at every moment), eventual consistency (it becomes consistent once modifications stop).

BASE trades a moment of inconsistency for an answer that always comes. As an example added here: for a like counter that is a good deal, for a bank balance it is not, which is one reason relational databases did not disappear.

Four ways to store one customer

NoSQL is a family, not a product. The lecture groups it into four types by how they represent data.

A key-value store keeps sets of key-value pairs, with four basic operations: add, remove, modify, find. A document database refines it: each record is a document in JSON or XML (two text formats that write data as labeled, nestable fields), which carries both metadata (the keys) and data (the values), with no schema constraints. A column-oriented database, derived from Bigtable (Google's distributed storage system), organizes data by column and lets each row hold a variable number of columns. A graph database stores nodes and the relationships between them, with a flexible schema.

Why store by column?

Organising data by column instead of by row is not exclusive to NoSQL: Oracle Exadata, the SQL Server column store index, SAP HANA and in-memory OLAP engines such as Power Pivot use it too. Its strengths are not wasting space when a value does not exist for a column and, often, a high degree of compression: a column holds values of one type, frequently repeated.

The lecture shows two algorithms. The first is dictionary encoding: list the distinct values once, in a dictionary, and replace each value with its position in the list. With \(\kk\) distinct values each code needs

\[ \text{bits per code} = \lceil \log_2 \kk \rceil \]

where the brackets mean rounding up; the slide shows the case of 4 values and 2 bits, the general formula is added here. The lecture's Year column has \(\kk = 4\) distinct years, so 2 bits per value: 00, 01, 10, 11.

The second is run-length encoding: replace each run of identical consecutive values with one triple (value, initial position, count). On the slide a Year column of 1,999 rows becomes four triples: (0, 1, 549), (1, 550, 450), (2, 1000, 500), (3, 1500, 500). The Customer column next to it, sorted within each year, starts (1, 1, 4), (2, 5, 3), and for 2010 (1, 550, 2), (2, 552, 2).

Run-length encoding compresses most when there are many identical values and the column is sorted. In the column stores of the NoSQL world, data are kept as key/value (or column/value) pairs grouped under a unique key, which the slide calls the primary key, so each row can hold values for any subset of columns.

HBase is the Apache column-oriented database inspired by Bigtable. It belongs to the Hadoop ecosystem and stores its data on HDFS, Hadoop's file system (lesson 5). It can be queried in SQL through Apache Phoenix (open source, a subset of ANSI SQL, the standard version of the language), Hive (part of the Hadoop ecosystem) or Splice Machine (a commercial product with standard SQL and transactions, suited to OLTP, the transactional use of lesson 2). The slide also shows HBase's own shell, where a scan or get command reads rows by key.

When the relationships are the data

In mathematics a graph is a set of nodes (vertices) connected by edges. Graph databases are designed to represent and navigate graphs efficiently, with no rigid schema.

Property graph. Nodes are containers of properties; properties are key/value pairs; relationships (edges) connect nodes and, in some implementations, carry properties too. Nodes represent objects (nouns), relationships represent actions (verbs) and have a direction.

The lecture's example is Neo4j, an open source graph database with ACID transactions, scalability to billions of nodes and excellent performance when traversing nodes. It is reached through a Java API (a programming interface), a console or a web interface, and queried in its own language, Cypher. The lecture's demo first shows all the nodes and relationships of a sample movie database, then asks for the films directed by one person, with all their actors.

MATCH (d:Person {name: 'Ava Reyes'})-[:DIRECTED]->(m:Movie)<-[:ACTED_IN]-(a:Person)
RETURN m.title, a.name

In a relational database the same question joins people to a directing table to films, and films back to people through an acting table. A graph database follows the edges directly, which is the node traversal the lecture lists among Neo4j's strengths.

Searching by meaning: vector databases

An embedding is a numerical vector produced by a machine learning model from a piece of content: a text, an image, a sound, a product. Every object becomes a vector of the same length (768 numbers in the lecture's example), and the model learns the coordinates so that similar meanings land close together. The single numbers matter less than where a vector sits relative to the others.

A vector database stores these vectors, with an id and metadata such as source, category or date, and is optimized to find the vectors closest to a query. Closeness is measured by Euclidean distance, by the dot product or by cosine similarity, which looks only at the angle between two vectors. For a query vector \(\qv\) and a stored vector \(\vb\), it divides their dot product (the sum of the products of matching coordinates) by the product of their lengths:

\[ \cos(\qv,\vb) = \frac{\qv \cdot \vb}{\lVert \qv \rVert \, \lVert \vb \rVert} \]

Typical uses are semantic search (content with a similar meaning even without the same words), recommendations, and RAG (retrieval augmented generation) for language models: retrieving the most relevant documents to pass to a generative model. To stay fast with millions or billions of vectors these databases use ANN indexes (approximate nearest neighbor; HNSW and IVF are two common types), which explore only a promising part of the space and may trade a little accuracy for speed. HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world) builds a layered graph of neighbors; IVF (inverted file) groups vectors into clusters and searches only the closest ones.

The lecture's simplified flow has five steps: content (a text or an image) goes through the embedding model, becomes a record with id, vector and metadata, such as {"id": "doc_42", "vector": [0.12, -0.81, …], "metadata": {"source": "slide.pdf"}}, enters the ANN index, and is found by a similarity search that returns the top k neighbors, ranked by score. These databases handle millions or billions of vectors, with updates, deletions and persistence on disk or in the cloud.

How do you store a file bigger than any disk?

Hadoop, the Big Data "ecosystem": the HDFS distributed file system, the MapReduce computation engine, and the tools built on top of them.

Lesson 4 named the two main Big Data tools, Hadoop and Spark, and recalled the vocabulary of distributed systems: a node is a single server, a rack a case with a bunch of servers, a cluster a collection of servers, in one or more racks, that behaves as a single machine. This lesson opens Hadoop.

Hadoop. A distributed calculation system based on two components: HDFS (Hadoop Distributed File System), which stores the data, and MapReduce, a distributed calculation framework, which computes on them.

Hadoop is open source, distributed and scalable up to thousands of nodes, and handles both structured and unstructured data. It is also fault tolerant: it is built to run on commodity hardware, cheap ordinary servers that do break, and it survives their failures by keeping copies of the data. How?

Cut the file into blocks, then copy each block

Each file in HDFS is made of parts called blocks, generally 64 or 128 MB each. Each block is replicated: the default replication factor is 3, and it can be changed. The copies live on different nodes, so no single disk holds the whole file and no single failure destroys it.

A server called the name node holds all the file system metadata: the list of files, the list of blocks of each file, and their locations. It is the master node of the cluster: it handles operations such as creating, renaming and deleting files, and it takes care of replication. The other nodes are the data nodes, the slaves, which carry out the actual reads and writes.

What if a whole rack goes dark?

Replication protects the data until all the nodes holding copies of the same block fail. A rack shares power and network, so it can fail all at once: three copies on three nodes of the same rack would vanish together. That is why HDFS is rack aware: it places the copies so that even if an entire rack fails, all the data is still available.

Copies on different racks turn a hardware failure into a non-event. The price is disk space: with replication factor n, a file takes n times its size.

Hadoop 3 replaces replication with a more space efficient algorithm called erasure coding (EC). EC adds some extra data to the blocks, encoded so that the system can use it to rebuild other blocks if they become unavailable. According to the lecture it cuts the storage overhead, the extra space beyond the file itself, to 50%. A note on the slide: in Hadoop 3 erasure coding is an option enabled folder by folder, while replication with factor 3 remains the default.

Moving the computation to the data

Storing is half the job. MapReduce is the distributed computation engine that works on the data stored in HDFS. Today Hadoop has other, more efficient engines, but they all derive from MapReduce, which only has a Java API. The programmer must split the problem into two phases.

Map and reduce. In the map phase the data are prepared and mapped to a key/value structure: the output of the mapper program is a set of key/value pairs. In the reduce phase the actual calculation is performed: the output of the reducer is the final result, saved to an HDFS file.

The system does the rest. It runs the mapper on each node where parts of the input file are stored, so the computation travels to the data instead of the data to the computation. Then it sorts, shuffles and aggregates the mapper outputs, putting the values of the same key together, and runs the reducer on each node. The lecture's simplest example counts the letters of the input "A C B C A".

Word count is the first task of any text analysis, and the lecture runs it on a sentence from the Hadoop website. The mapper emits (word, 1) for every word; sort and aggregate collect the ones of each word; the reducer adds them up. Words are compared without capitals, so "The" and "the" are one key.

Built for batch, not for questions

MapReduce was designed for batch workloads: computations on a huge amount of data that complete in minutes or even hours. It is not suitable for interactive queries, the many small questions we send to an RDBMS (a relational database management system), where we expect an answer in seconds or tenths of a second. And it is hard to program: most problems are very difficult to express with a map and a reduce.

Pig and Hive make MapReduce easier. They offer high level languages and translate programs written in them into MapReduce jobs: Pig Latin is a procedural language whose instructions resemble SQL keywords, HiveQL an SQL dialect that implements the ANSI SQL syntax plus some built in functions. The main Hadoop distributions also provide ODBC drivers (a standard way for programs to connect to a database) that let users connect to Hive as if it were an RDBMS: every Business Intelligence and reporting tool can then reach Hadoop.

An ecosystem, not a program

Around HDFS and MapReduce grew a family of tools that cover the whole data life cycle. The lecture groups them in four layers.

The core services are HDFS, the engines MapReduce, Tez (which runs a job as a graph of steps rather than a fixed map and reduce) and Spark, and YARN (yet another resource negotiator), the software layer that allocates the cluster's resources to each Hadoop task and to the applications above it.

The data services include Hive, the SQL interface to Hadoop, which translates HiveQL into MapReduce, Tez or Spark programs; Pig, which does the same for Pig Latin; HBase, the NoSQL columnar database of lesson 4, which only works inside Hadoop; Storm and Flume, which ingest data produced in streams, such as server logs, tweets or IoT sensor readings; and Sqoop, a sort of ETL tool (extract, transform, load, lesson 1) between an RDBMS and Hadoop, which imports relational tables into Hadoop or exports data back. The diagram also shows HCatalog, which shares Hive's table definitions with the other tools (a gloss added here).

The operational services are Ambari, a web tool to manage the cluster; Oozie, a workflow tool to orchestrate jobs (MapReduce, Hive, Pig, Sqoop, Flume, Storm and so on); and Zookeeper, which shares and keeps synchronized the configuration files of each application.

The analytic tools are of three kinds. Some only work inside a Hadoop cluster: Giraph for graphs, Mahout for machine learning, Kylin for OLAP (the multidimensional analysis of lesson 1), which saves aggregations in HBase's columnar storage and uses them to answer Hive queries. Others are distributed tools of their own: Elastic Search, a powerful search engine, and Spark. Others connect to Hadoop from outside: SAS, KNIME (the tool of the second part of this course) and R.

Installing a cluster from the Apache distribution can be very hard, so companies use distributions with a semi-automatic installer; the most famous is Cloudera, which merged in 2018 with Hortonworks, another Hadoop distributor. On balance, Hadoop can handle every data type and store and compute huge amounts of data, but it is complex, difficult to install and maintain, and slow for some workloads such as interactive queries.

Is Hadoop still used?

Yes, but its role has shifted from default Big Data platform to installed enterprise infrastructure. It remains common in large organizations with mature on-premise or hybrid platforms (part in the company's own data centre, part in the cloud), especially where moving petabytes of data is expensive. MapReduce, however, is no longer central.

The new names, as the lecture defines them. Trino is a distributed SQL query engine that queries large data sets across different sources (S3, Hive, Iceberg, PostgreSQL and others) without moving them into one database. Flink is a distributed stream-processing engine that processes data continuously as events arrive, often in real time. Iceberg, created at Netflix, and Delta Lake, created by Databricks, the company of Spark's creators, are open table formats: they keep the data in files, usually Parquet, a columnar file format met again in lesson 6, plus metadata and transaction logs, so that several engines can treat the files as tables. Object storage means cloud services such as Amazon S3, Azure Blob Storage or ADLS Gen2, and Google Cloud Storage.

The usual Hadoop alternative is a data lake, or lakehouse, built with Spark on cloud storage: lesson 8 is about it. The lecture's advice to a data engineer in 2026 sums up the shift.

What makes Spark so fast?

Lightning fast computing with Spark: in-memory processing, the cluster architecture, RDDs, transformations and actions, DataFrames and Spark SQL.

Lesson 5 ended with MapReduce: map close to the data, shuffle, reduce, and a result written to HDFS. It works, but it is slow and hard to program. Spark does the same kind of job, and the first slide of the lecture says how it differs.

Spark. A fast, in-memory data processing engine, with APIs in Scala, Java, Python and R. Running on Hadoop's YARN, it integrates deeply with Hadoop; on top of its core it powers a stack of high level tools: Spark SQL, MLlib for machine learning, GraphX for graphs and Spark Streaming.

Spark started in 2009 as a Hadoop subproject at AMPLab, became open source in 2010 and an Apache project in 2013; versions 1.6.2 and 2.0.0 came in 2016, 2.3 and 2.4 in 2018, 3.x in 2020. The slide places AMPLab at UCLA: it is the laboratory of the University of California at Berkeley.

Keep the data in memory between steps

Most real jobs have several steps: clean, then join, then aggregate, then iterate. MapReduce reads its input from HDFS and writes its output to HDFS at every step, and disks are slow. Spark reads once, keeps the intermediate results in memory and writes only at the end.

Spark is fast because it stops writing to disk between one step and the next. Not only: the lecture stresses that it is fast for on-disk computation too, as two benchmarks show.

Who does what in a Spark cluster

The driver program contains all the instructions Spark must execute, for example read a file, aggregate the data, save the result to another file. In it the programmer creates the SparkSession object, which connects to the cluster through a cluster manager and sends the program to be executed. Spark can use YARN as cluster manager if it lives on the same cluster as Hadoop, Apache Mesos, or its own standalone manager.

Once connected, the driver sends the lines of code with the Spark commands. The worker nodes, generally one per physical machine, receive them and allocate one or more executors: services running on the Java virtual machine (JVM) that create the tasks which physically execute the instructions, and allocate enough memory to each task. Executors on different workers exchange data when needed: these exchanges are the shuffles, and they happen when the program aggregates data (rows with the same key may sit on different workers) or sorts them.

The master parameter of the session decides which cluster to use, as in SparkSession.builder.master("spark://myserver:7077").appName("myApp").getOrCreate().

Data cut into partitions: the RDD

RDD. A resilient distributed dataset is the primary abstraction of Spark: an immutable, fault-tolerant collection of elements, cut into partitions that are distributed across the nodes of the cluster and processed in parallel.

Each partition is managed by an executor, generally one executor per node, and an executor can manage more than one partition of the same RDD. An RDD can hold strings, as when you read a text file, or key/value pairs, created by the program. It is created either from a file system (HDFS, HBase, or any Hadoop data source) or by parallelizing an existing Scala or Python collection.

Nothing happens until you ask for a result

Once an RDD exists, every operation on it is one of two kinds. A transformation creates a new RDD from an existing one and adds a step to the processing plan, the DAG (directed acyclic graph: the execution workflow of the program). An action executes the DAG and returns a value. Transformations alone compute nothing: Spark waits for an action, then runs the whole plan at once.

rdd = sc.textFile("input.txt")\
    .flatMap(lambda line: line.split())\
    .map(lambda word: (word, 1))\
    .reduceByKey(lambda x, y: x + y, 3)\
    .collect()

Read line by line: sc is the SparkContext of the session; textFile reads the file into an RDD of lines, flatMap splits the lines into one big RDD of words, map turns each word into a key/value pair (word, 1), reduceByKey groups by key and sums the values into 3 partitions, and collect returns the result to the driver. A lambda is a small function written inline.

A DAG is cut into stages at every shuffle, and the stages run in order. Inside a stage each partition goes through its steps on its own executor; at the boundary the data must be redistributed, so that all the pairs of one word meet in the same partition.

Tables again: DataFrames and Spark SQL

To Spark the elements of an RDD are opaque objects: their inner structure is unknown. Spark SQL is the module for structured data, and it adds the DataFrame: a distributed collection of data organized into named columns, conceptually equivalent to a table of a relational database or to a data frame in R or Python, with richer optimizations under the hood. It can also act as a distributed SQL query engine.

An RDD becomes a DataFrame in two ways, both in the course script: by declaring a schema (StructType with the field names and types) or by turning each element into a Row and letting Spark infer the schema. DataFrames are also read directly from data sources such as JSON files, JDBC connections (the Java standard for reaching a database) to relational databases, or Parquet files, an optimized format that stores data in columns together with their metadata (column names and data types).

The course script loads sales.csv, eleven sales with date, product, value and customer id, and works on it with the DataFrame API and with SQL:

sales = spark.read.option("header", True).option("inferSchema", True).csv("sales.csv")
sales.select(sales["product"], sales["value"]).show(2)
sales.filter(sales["value"] > 200).show()
sales.groupBy("product").sum("value").show()
sales.createOrReplaceTempView("sales")
spark.sql("select product, sum(value) as qt_tot from sales group by product")

A DataFrame registered with createOrReplaceTempView becomes a temporary table that lives as long as the session; spark.catalog.dropTempView removes it. saveAsTable instead materializes the DataFrame as a persistent table in the Hive metastore, the catalog where Hive keeps its table definitions (Spark creates a local one, based on the Derby database, if no Hive installation exists): the table survives restarts as long as you connect to the same metastore.

How does Spark handle data that never stops arriving?

The rest of the Spark stack: Structured Streaming for live data, GraphX and GraphFrames for graphs, MLlib and its pipelines for machine learning.

Lesson 6 built Spark from the core up: partitioned data, transformations, actions, DataFrames and Spark SQL. On top of that core sit three more components: Structured Streaming for stream processing, GraphX for graph computation and MLlib for machine learning. This lesson takes them one at a time.

A table that never ends

Streaming data is data continuously generated by a source: clicks on a website, sensor readings, transactions. Such data should be processed incrementally: you cannot wait for the stream to end, because it never does.

Structured Streaming. The Spark component for scalable, high-throughput, fault-tolerant processing of live data streams. Built on the Spark SQL engine, it lets you express a streaming computation exactly as you would express a batch computation on static data; the engine runs it incrementally and continuously, updating the final result as new data arrive.

The trick, in the Spark documentation that the lecture shows, is to treat the stream as an unbounded input table: every new piece of data is a new row appended to it. A query on that table produces a result table, and at every trigger (a moment when Spark checks for new data) the result table is updated and written out. The documentation's example counts words in lines of text.

A stream is a table that keeps growing, and the query is written once. At time 3 the engine does not restart from scratch: it combines the new lines with the counts it already has.

The course notebook does the same on about 50 JSON files of events, each with a time and an action ("Open" or "Close"). First it counts the actions in one-hour windows with a normal, static query; then it replays the files as a stream, one file per trigger, with the same query:

streamingInputDF = (spark.readStream.schema(jsonSchema)
    .option("maxFilesPerTrigger", 1).json(inputPath))
streamingCountsDF = (streamingInputDF
    .groupBy(streamingInputDF.action, window(streamingInputDF.time, "1 hour"))
    .count())
query = (streamingCountsDF.writeStream.format("memory")
    .queryName("counts").outputMode("complete").start())

The only change from the static version is readStream in place of read. window(time, "1 hour") groups the events by the hour in which they happened; the sink is an in-memory table called counts, for testing, and outputMode("complete") keeps all the counts in it. Querying counts with SQL a few seconds apart shows the numbers growing as files are read.

When the relationships are the data

Some data are about connections: who is friends with whom in a social network, which web page links to which, which user bought which item. The natural structure is a graph, made of nodes (the entities, also called vertices) and relationships (the connections, also called edges).

Property graph. A graph whose nodes can hold any number of attributes (key/value pairs called properties) and can be tagged with labels for their roles, and whose relationships are directed, named and semantically relevant: each has a direction, a type, a start node and an end node.

GraphX is the Spark component for graphs and graph-parallel computation. It extends the RDD with a Graph abstraction: a directed multigraph (two vertices may be linked by more than one edge) with properties attached to each vertex and edge. It also includes a collection of graph algorithms and builders; the built-in algorithms named in the lecture are PageRank, which identifies important vertices from their connections, connected components, which finds the groups of vertices linked to each other, and triangle count, the number of triangles passing through each vertex.

GraphFrames is a library that simplifies the GraphX API and lets you use DataFrames instead of RDDs: a graph is a vertex DataFrame, with a column named id, plus an edge DataFrame, with columns src and dst. The course notebook builds a small social graph with it.

The notebook then asks the graph four kinds of questions. The degree of a vertex is the number of its edges, split into in-degree (edges arriving) and out-degree (edges leaving). Because vertices and edges are DataFrames, ordinary queries also work: the youngest person is 29 (David), and four edges are of type "follow". A subgraph keeps only some edges and vertices: the notebook keeps the "friend" edges between people older than 30, then drops the vertices left without edges.

A graph algorithm answers a question about structure that no single row could answer. Gabby's isolation, or the triangle among Alice, David and Esther, only appear when you follow the edges.

Machine learning on DataFrames

MLlib is Spark's machine learning library; its goal is to make practical machine learning scalable and easy. It provides learning algorithms (classification, regression, clustering, collaborative filtering), featurization (feature extraction, transformation, dimensionality reduction and selection), pipelines to build, evaluate and tune machine learning workflows, and utilities such as linear algebra and statistics.

The typical machine learning process, as the lecture describes it, starts by understanding the data and splitting them into a training set and a test set. Then the data are prepared: cleansed, and adapted to the algorithm, for example normalized or with categorical variables converted to numbers. The algorithm is trained on the training set, and the model is tested on the test set, data it has never seen.

The ML Pipeline API standardizes these pieces so they can be assembled like a production line, and the data set flowing through it is simply a DataFrame. A pipeline has three kinds of components.

Transformer, estimator, evaluator. A transformer turns a DataFrame into another DataFrame, usually by adding a column (Tokenizer, Binarizer, OneHotEncoder). An estimator is a learning algorithm: it fits a model on a DataFrame and produces a transformer, the model, that can calculate predictions for any DataFrame. An evaluator takes a DataFrame of predictions and computes metrics of how good the model is.

The slide calls the evaluator a transformer; in Spark's API it is a component of its own, which reads a DataFrame and returns a number rather than a new DataFrame.

The course's first example classifies toy sentences as being about baking (label 1) or about travel (label 0). The training set has four sentences: "sugar flour eggs" and "flour yeast salt" with label 1, "mountain trip" and "sea beach" with label 0. The pipeline has three stages.

Tokenizer lowercases each sentence and splits it on spaces into words. HashingTF turns the words into a vector of 1,000 numbers: each word is sent by a hash function (a fixed scrambling of its letters into a number) to one of 1,000 positions, which counts how many times it occurs. LogisticRegression, run for at most 10 iterations with a small penalty on large weights (regParam 0.01), learns one weight per position.

To read the predictions, recall how logistic regression decides: it adds the weights of the words in the sentence to a constant, the intercept, and turns this score \(z\) into a probability with the logistic function \(p = 1/(1+e^{-z})\); a probability above 0.5 predicts label 1. Running the course's script (we used Spark 3.5) gives an intercept of −1.14 and these weights: +2.28 for flour, which appears in both baking sentences, +1.52 for sugar, eggs, yeast and salt, −1.52 for mountain, trip, sea and beach, and 0 for the positions that no training word reaches. A test word gets the weight of the position it is hashed to.

A pipeline packs the preparation and the model together, so the test data go through exactly the steps the training data went through. The second example of the course is a linear regression that predicts the 2015 median house price of US cities from their 2014 population: a VectorAssembler (a transformer) packs the population into a features column, LinearRegression fits the model, and a RegressionEvaluator measures its error. The course materials also include a decision tree for fraud detection and notebooks on MLflow and generative AI, which the slides do not cover.

Where do the raw data go?

Big Data architectures: the data lake, how it differs from the data warehouse, how the two work together in the company's information system, and the newer idea of data virtualization.

Lessons 5 to 7 built the toolbox: HDFS to store huge files cheaply, Spark to process them, streaming, graphs and machine learning on top. Lesson 1 described the classic home of company data, the data warehouse (DWH): structured, cleaned data loaded by ETL processes (extract, transform, load) and ready for fast queries. Where do the new tools fit next to it? The lecture's answer is the data lake.

Gartner, a research and advisory firm, defines it as a collection of storage instances holding data assets in addition to the original sources, kept in a near-exact or exact copy of the source format. Its purpose is to give the most skilled analysts an unrefined view of the data, so they can explore refinement and analysis techniques free of the compromises built into a data warehouse or a data mart.

Data lake. In the lecturer's own definition, a repository that holds a vast amount of raw data in its native format, structured, semi-structured and unstructured, together with a vast amount of processed or semi-processed data. The structure is not defined until the data are needed (schema on read). It provides data for reporting, analytics and machine learning, and it is mostly based on Hadoop and Spark.

The raw data come from everywhere: web and clickstream, machine logs, devices and sensors, images, audio and video, social networks, relational databases, healthcare and financial data. Cleansing, aggregation and governance turn part of them into processed data, stored in the lake too. The technologies follow the life of the data.

The lecture names four kinds of users of the lake: data scientists, analysts, applications and business users.

Decide the schema before or after?

The deepest difference from a warehouse is when the structure is decided. A DWH uses schema on write: before inserting data we must define the tables, and every row must fit them. A data lake writes to a file system, so the schema, if needed, is defined when the data are read.

Schema on write protects the warehouse; schema on read keeps everything for later. The lecture sums up the comparison in five points.

Hot data are the more recent or more accessed data; cold data the older or less accessed ones. Cost is where the lake wins most clearly, as a chart from Hortonworks, the Hadoop distributor of lesson 5, shows.

A lake is adopted one step at a time

Companies rarely start with a lake at the centre of everything. The lecture describes four steps, each building on the one below.

Read from the base, the lake starts as a cheap place where data from the operational systems land, separate from everything else; it then becomes a laboratory for data scientists; then the warehouses lean on it to keep their cold data, thanks to its low storage costs; and finally most of the company's data flows pass through it.

The warehouse stays: who does what

The lake does not replace the warehouse; the architecture changes around it. In traditional Business Intelligence (BI), data from the sources go through ETL into the DWH, and a semantic layer (the business view of the data: measures, dimensions, names users understand) feeds the reports. In what the lecture calls BI 2.0, emerging sources join the traditional ones and the lake sits next to the warehouse.

The data flows change accordingly. Data from the traditional sources can still go through ETL, but some or all of the ETL procedures can move into the lake, which becomes the repository of raw data and the place where transformations happen. Data from the new sources go directly to the lake, where they can be processed or used in complex analytical tasks such as machine learning; the results can then be moved to the warehouse.

The warehouse still holds the hot data coming from the ETL processes, whether they read the sources directly or the lake, and the distilled data coming from the analyses run on the lake. It allows low-latency, interactive queries and keeps the reporting systems built on it working. The lake holds all the raw data and the final processed data; the hot part is pushed to the warehouse, while the cold part stays in the lake, available with a longer latency. With more detail and a longer history, it supplies the warehouse, data scientists (for both descriptive reports and predictive models) and corporate applications.

The lecture calls the movement from lake to warehouse data distillation: the lake allows more detailed and complex analyses on much more data, and the results are often small enough to be transferred to the warehouse and injected into the BI workflow.

A lake takes whatever you give it

That is its strength and its problem. Without rules a lake fills with data nobody can find or trust, and many users with different profiles must be able to use it. The lecture names four critical success factors: data governance and a data quality process against the first problem, metadata management (part of governance) and training of data scientists and end users against the second.

A data governance policy maps the data sources (applications, input services), the data flows (how data are loaded into the lake) and the output services (the warehouse, applications, output data services), and manages the metadata, the data about the data, which make it easy to search the lake and interpret its content.

The data quality process has a less obvious rule: quality rules should be applied to the output data, not to the input. A filter at the entrance would keep out data of medium quality that could still be useful for certain applications.

Label the quality and let the final user decide. That is the lecture's recommendation: put a quality label on the data rather than filtering them out.

Or leave the data where they are

A newer trend avoids copying altogether. Data virtualization is a logical data layer that integrates the enterprise data siloed in different systems, manages them in one place for security and governance, and delivers them to business users in real time.

The pros, according to the lecture: cost savings, since storing and maintaining data is cheaper than replicating them and transforming them into other formats and places; logical abstraction, since heterogeneous sources can interact more easily; easier governance, since rules are applied centrally to all the data; a bridge between structured and unstructured data; and higher productivity, since less time goes into integrating sources. The cons follow from the second panel: it relies on the storage, memory and CPUs of the sources, can require big memory and computation resources, increases network traffic, needs a high initial investment and can be slow for complex tasks.

Predict or generate?

Predictive AI uses historical data and traditional machine learning to find patterns and make predictions about future events. It is not magic: it rests on probabilities and correlations found in the data, so every prediction comes with a level of uncertainty. According to the lecture it can include descriptive analysis (what happened?), diagnostic analysis (why did it happen?) and prescriptive analysis (what should we do?). Generative AI differs in one ability: it creates new content, such as text, images, code or audio.

Predictive and generative models. A predictive (or discriminative) model learns a function that predicts a target variable from the characteristics of the observations, the input variables (also called predictors or features). A generative model captures the main characteristics of the data and the links among them, with the goal of generating entirely new data.

Generative AI is growing fast and creates new use cases, but according to the estimates of the consulting firm McKinsey, quoted in the lecture, predictive AI is still the primary source of value.

From reports to agents

Lesson 1 told the history of analysis in five phases. The lecture's version has eight, and its milestones show where the focus moved: from tools to applications, to data, to natural language, to autonomous action.

The Business Intelligence process behind all of it has four moves: systematically collect the data assets the company generates, cleanse them (the data quality process), transform data into information, and present it in a simple, usable form such as reports and dashboards. In technical terms: extract from ERP, CRM, files, web and IoT; cleanse and transform with quality checks, integration and business rules; load into the data warehouse; analyse and disseminate through reports, OLAP, data mining and machine learning. Two things are new today: in cloud platforms the order often becomes ELT (data are loaded raw and transformed inside the warehouse or lakehouse), and dashboards are increasingly queried in natural language, through AI copilots on top of BI.

The governance layer covers master data management (one agreed version of key entities such as customers and products), data quality, metadata and lineage (where each figure comes from), security and privacy under the GDPR, the EU's data protection regulation.

Garbage in, garbage out

Why should someone who builds models care about all this? Because however correct and refined a model is, it can never overcome inaccurate or distorted input. Upstream of the models there must be a robust, reliable data environment: a single, comprehensive and consistent container of data from heterogeneous sources, built for the end users' analyses. That container is the data warehouse. In 2026 the same rule applies to AI: language models and agents are only as good as the data they are grounded on.

Data warehousing. The process of collecting and managing data from different corporate sources to serve the business applications. In the 1996 definition of Bill Inmon, often called the father of the data warehouse, the result is integrated, subject-oriented, non-volatile and time-variant.

In larger companies the central warehouse can be split into data marts: smaller environments fed by the warehouse, each an aggregation or selection of its data for one business function, such as marketing or finance. A modern evolution is the data mesh, where each domain owns and publishes its own "data products" on a shared platform. The warehouse also needs its metadata: type, origin, meaning, use, owner, refresh date and lineage of every datum.

How big is Big Data?

Since 2016, analysis has rested on three pillars: Big Data (new versions of Hadoop, then Spark, and today cloud-native engines and serverless SQL), predictive analytics (extracting knowledge from data that seem to have no structure, and using it to predict; today AutoML, which automates model choice and tuning, and MLOps, the practices to deploy and monitor models, run them at scale) and prescriptive analytics (rules directly applicable to the business, built on predictions; today optimisation and AI agents that act). The lecture shows the rise of interest in machine learning with Google Trends charts, in the world and in Italy, which are not reproduced here.

The data come from two families of sources. The traditional ones are the operational systems of daily activity, today mostly cloud ERP and CRM suites: production, purchasing, orders and deliveries, accounting, HR, customers, financial instruments. They are structured, reliable and well documented, and still the main input of most predictive models. The emergent ones are closer to production, people and the physical world: the Internet of Things, edge computing (data processed close to where they are produced), social media and user-generated content, vehicles and drones, health wearables, satellite images, blockchain transactions, genomic data, augmented and virtual reality, chatbot conversation logs, synthetic data generated to train models while protecting privacy, and quantum computing, which may open new ways to solve complex data problems.

Data are structured when they fit a table of rows and columns (relational databases, transactions, spreadsheets), semi-structured when they have tags or keys but no rigid schema (JSON, XML, logs, e-mail headers), and unstructured when they have no predefined structure (documents, images, audio, video, posts, call transcripts). An estimated 80 to 90% of new enterprise data is unstructured, and language models now make it usable as model input.

The five Vs. The classical definition describes Big Data by volume (from terabytes to petabytes and beyond), variety (of structures, data types and sources) and velocity (the speed at which data are produced and must be processed: streaming, real time). Two more Vs complete it: value, the ability to generate value measured on business KPIs, and veracity, because not everything on the internet or in the company's data is trustworthy.

Do we have to analyse them all? No. The lecture calls the name and the classical definition misleading on three counts: Big Data are not only volume (variety and velocity often matter more), not only the web (they also come from IoT, apps, and from traditional ERP and CRM systems) and not all of them (the impressive statistics count every datum in the world; what matters are the relevant ones). Hence the definition by Paolo Pasini of SDA Bocconi, release 1.0:

Big Data. A new concept of potential business knowledge that leverages today's variety of formats and sources, the improved velocity and, as a consequence, today's growing volume, to generate insights previously beyond our capability and to find new business value through proper enablers.

The enablers are data quality (veracity), security and privacy; BI, analytics and database platforms; cloud services; and management culture with new skills. Complexity, not just size, decides how hard data are to use: it depends on the type of structure, the presence of metadata, accessibility and difficulty of interpretation.

Four triggers made the phenomenon possible: data availability from many sources, open-source tools that lowered the entry barrier (Hadoop, Spark, Python, R, KNIME), cheap and elastic processing power (multi-core, cloud, GPUs and AI accelerators on demand), and falling storage costs, with cloud object storage making it cheap to keep everything. Three lessons run through the cases. Always compare against a baseline. Zalando's forecasting model is a transformer, a deep learning architecture, but a simpler model is a strong benchmark.

The second lesson is ethics and law. Target's pregnancy score became a textbook case of inferring sensitive information that customers never disclosed: in the EU today such inferences touch special categories of data (health) under the GDPR and need explicit consent. The EU AI Act classifies AI for recruitment and HR decisions as high-risk, which matters for attrition models like IBM's. And the Revenue Agency's algorithms never trigger an assessment automatically: a human evaluates each case, with pseudonymisation and data minimisation required by the Italian Data Protection Authority.
