Where do the raw data go?

Big Data architectures: the data lake, how it differs from the data warehouse, how the two work together in the company's information system, and the newer idea of data virtualization.

Lessons 5 to 7 built the toolbox: HDFS to store huge files cheaply, Spark to process them, streaming, graphs and machine learning on top. Lesson 1 described the classic home of company data, the data warehouse (DWH): structured, cleaned data loaded by ETL processes (extract, transform, load) and ready for fast queries. Where do the new tools fit next to it? The lecture's answer is the data lake.

Gartner, a research and advisory firm, defines it as a collection of storage instances holding data assets in addition to the original sources, kept in a near-exact or exact copy of the source format. Its purpose is to give the most skilled analysts an unrefined view of the data, so they can explore refinement and analysis techniques free of the compromises built into a data warehouse or a data mart.

Data lake. In the lecturer's own definition, a repository that holds a vast amount of raw data in its native format, structured, semi-structured and unstructured, together with a vast amount of processed or semi-processed data. The structure is not defined until the data are needed (schema on read). It provides data for reporting, analytics and machine learning, and it is mostly based on Hadoop and Spark.

The raw data come from everywhere: web and clickstream, machine logs, devices and sensors, images, audio and video, social networks, relational databases, healthcare and financial data. Cleansing, aggregation and governance turn part of them into processed data, stored in the lake too. The technologies follow the life of the data.

The lecture names four kinds of users of the lake: data scientists, analysts, applications and business users.

Decide the schema before or after?

The deepest difference from a warehouse is when the structure is decided. A DWH uses schema on write: before inserting data we must define the tables, and every row must fit them. A data lake writes to a file system, so the schema, if needed, is defined when the data are read.

Schema on write protects the warehouse; schema on read keeps everything for later. The lecture sums up the comparison in five points.

Hot data are the more recent or more accessed data; cold data the older or less accessed ones. Cost is where the lake wins most clearly, as a chart from Hortonworks, the Hadoop distributor of lesson 5, shows.

A lake is adopted one step at a time

Companies rarely start with a lake at the centre of everything. The lecture describes four steps, each building on the one below.

Read from the base, the lake starts as a cheap place where data from the operational systems land, separate from everything else; it then becomes a laboratory for data scientists; then the warehouses lean on it to keep their cold data, thanks to its low storage costs; and finally most of the company's data flows pass through it.

The warehouse stays: who does what

The lake does not replace the warehouse; the architecture changes around it. In traditional Business Intelligence (BI), data from the sources go through ETL into the DWH, and a semantic layer (the business view of the data: measures, dimensions, names users understand) feeds the reports. In what the lecture calls BI 2.0, emerging sources join the traditional ones and the lake sits next to the warehouse.

The data flows change accordingly. Data from the traditional sources can still go through ETL, but some or all of the ETL procedures can move into the lake, which becomes the repository of raw data and the place where transformations happen. Data from the new sources go directly to the lake, where they can be processed or used in complex analytical tasks such as machine learning; the results can then be moved to the warehouse.

The warehouse still holds the hot data coming from the ETL processes, whether they read the sources directly or the lake, and the distilled data coming from the analyses run on the lake. It allows low-latency, interactive queries and keeps the reporting systems built on it working. The lake holds all the raw data and the final processed data; the hot part is pushed to the warehouse, while the cold part stays in the lake, available with a longer latency. With more detail and a longer history, it supplies the warehouse, data scientists (for both descriptive reports and predictive models) and corporate applications.

The lecture calls the movement from lake to warehouse data distillation: the lake allows more detailed and complex analyses on much more data, and the results are often small enough to be transferred to the warehouse and injected into the BI workflow.

A lake takes whatever you give it

That is its strength and its problem. Without rules a lake fills with data nobody can find or trust, and many users with different profiles must be able to use it. The lecture names four critical success factors: data governance and a data quality process against the first problem, metadata management (part of governance) and training of data scientists and end users against the second.

A data governance policy maps the data sources (applications, input services), the data flows (how data are loaded into the lake) and the output services (the warehouse, applications, output data services), and manages the metadata, the data about the data, which make it easy to search the lake and interpret its content.

The data quality process has a less obvious rule: quality rules should be applied to the output data, not to the input. A filter at the entrance would keep out data of medium quality that could still be useful for certain applications.

Label the quality and let the final user decide. That is the lecture's recommendation: put a quality label on the data rather than filtering them out.

Or leave the data where they are

A newer trend avoids copying altogether. Data virtualization is a logical data layer that integrates the enterprise data siloed in different systems, manages them in one place for security and governance, and delivers them to business users in real time.

The pros, according to the lecture: cost savings, since storing and maintaining data is cheaper than replicating them and transforming them into other formats and places; logical abstraction, since heterogeneous sources can interact more easily; easier governance, since rules are applied centrally to all the data; a bridge between structured and unstructured data; and higher productivity, since less time goes into integrating sources. The cons follow from the second panel: it relies on the storage, memory and CPUs of the sources, can require big memory and computation resources, increases network traffic, needs a high initial investment and can be slow for complex tasks.
